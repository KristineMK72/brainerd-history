A history of the crossing

Brainerd was built where the railroad met the river.

Before it was a lakes town, before Paul Bunyan sat on a stump and talked to children, this was Ojibwe ground and then a Northern Pacific machine shop in the pines. The story of Brainerd is the story of a crossing that refused to stay small.

Chapter I

A river already named

The land that became Brainerd was Ojibwe territory — hunting ground, blueberry field, and a working stretch of the Mississippi long before surveyors arrived with chain and compass. Crow Wing Village, a few miles downstream near Fort Ripley, was the earlier gathering place: furs, logs, traders, and families living in a complicated borderland.

European-American explorers first wrote the site into their journals on Christmas Day, 1805, when Zebulon Pike paused here looking for the river’s headwaters. The town itself would wait another sixty-five years. What changed everything was not a homestead. It was a railroad that needed to get across the Mississippi on its way to the Pacific.

Chapter II · 1870

Named in a boardroom

In 1870 Northern Pacific president John Gregory Smith chose a crossing seven miles north of Crow Wing. Hundreds of workers poured into a jack-pine clearing. For a few weeks the place was called Ogemaqua — from an Ojibwe name associated with a prominent local woman — and, more bluntly, The Crossing.

On October 1, 1870, the railroad’s directors renamed it Brainerd. Popular memory says Smith honored his wife, Ann Eliza Brainerd Smith. A later Brainerd Tribune interview quoted Smith saying the name honored her father, Lawrence Brainerd, the Vermont senator and railroad partner. Both names stuck to the map: the town for the family, the central park for Gregory.

The wooden trestle opened March 8, 1871. Engineer Adam Brown brought the first passenger train across. Crow Wing emptied as people followed the shops north. Brainerd was organized as a city on March 6, 1873.

The railroad town

Shops, saws, and a tower like a flashlight

The Northern Pacific did not just pass through. It built a division point: roundhouse, machine shops, car shops, a hospital, a tie plant. By the 1920s roughly nine in ten Brainerd households depended on the railroad. More than a thousand people worked the shops that kept locomotives alive for a line stretching toward Puget Sound.

Timber rode the same rails. Boom Lake earned its name from the log booms that fed mills such as J. J. Howe & Co., which could ship 15 to 25 million feet of lumber a year — and burned, more than once, because a mill yard is a fortune stacked as kindling. In 1903 Northwest Paper added pulp and newsprint. The forest was no longer only scenery. It was payroll.

Chapter III

Years the town still tells

1872 · The so-called Blueberry War

Fear, a lynching, and a harvest

After a girl disappeared near Crow Wing, two Ojibwe men were accused of murder, seized, and killed by a mob. Their guilt was never proven. The governor sent militia from Fort Ripley, expecting a reprisal. What arrived instead were Ojibwe families coming to sell blueberries. The nickname stuck; the injustice did too.

July 27, 1875

The Crossing collapses

Four years after it opened, the wooden Mississippi trestle gave way under a freight train. Engine and cars dropped into the river. The engineer, the fireman, and two Ojibwe women passengers from Sawyer were killed. A railroad town lives with risk in its foundations.

1876–1881

A city without a charter

Voters chose Thomas Lanihan, a local handyman, over Judge C. B. Sleeper. The legislature revoked Brainerd’s city charter for six years. The place kept running as a township until the railroad boom of the early 1880s made the argument look small.

July 1, 1922

When the shops went quiet

Brainerd joined the national railroad shopmen’s strike. In a city whose economy was the Northern Pacific, unemployment was not a sector problem. It was the town. Efficiency and shops in Livingston, Montana, later finished what the strike began.

October 23, 1933

Baby Face in the lobby

Lester “Baby Face” Nelson’s gang spent the morning inside the First National Bank at Sixth and Front. They waited for the time lock, filled canvas bags with about $32,000, and left firing a submachine gun into the empty lobby. Clerk Zane Smith, 19, was punched on his way in. He survived to tell the story for seventy years.

1950 · and after

From sawlogs to summers

As the shops shrank, the lakes took over the letterhead. A talking Paul Bunyan from the Chicago Railroad Fair became the region’s mascot. Donnybrooke Speedway opened in 1968 and became BIR, hosting Can-Am, Paul Newman, and later NHRA nationals. Paper lingered. Tourism stayed.

Folklore

The giant who advertised the woods

Paul Bunyan was not born in Brainerd. The camp tales grew up in Great Lakes logging country and were popularized by Minnesota advertising man William Laughead for the Red River Lumber Company. What Brainerd did was give the giant a job in tourism.

In 1949 local buyers brought a 26-foot mechanized Paul home from the Chicago Railroad Fair. Paul Bunyan Land opened in 1950. The statue learned children’s names. A smaller Paul still waves from the Brainerd Lakes Welcome Center. The historic water tower — crenellated, concrete, slightly improbable — picked up the nickname Paul Bunyan’s flashlight, or his golf tee.

The legend is affectionate and incomplete. It celebrates the lumberjack and says little about the Ojibwe homelands the mills overran, or the white pine that did not grow back at the same scale. Both stories belong on the same map.

“The Big Fellow has settled down at last… Paul Bunyan has chosen Brainerd for his home.”

Brainerd Dispatch, October 12, 1949

Faces of the crossing

People the town remembers

“Father of Brainerd”

Lyman P. White

Civil War quartermaster and land-company agent who arrived by stage in 1870, platted lots, and later served as mayor. He helped turn a construction camp into a town.

Namesake

Lawrence Brainerd

Vermont senator, railroad man, and anti-slavery organizer. Local research argues the town was named for him at an October 1870 Northern Pacific board meeting — not only for his daughter, Ann Eliza Brainerd Smith.

Railroad president

J. Gregory Smith

Northern Pacific’s first president chose the river crossing and rejected “Smithville.” Gregory Square still carries his middle name.

Governor of Minnesota

C. Elmer Anderson

Born in Brainerd in 1912 to Finnish immigrant parents, he rose from paperboy to lieutenant governor and, in 1951–55, the state’s 28th governor. He later served as Brainerd’s mayor.

Hall of Fame pitcher

Charles Albert Bender

Born in Crow Wing County in 1884 to a German father and Ojibwe mother. Winner of 212 major-league games, three World Series titles, and a pioneer of the pitch that became the slider. Elected to Cooperstown in 1953.

World Series pitcher

Leslie “Bullet Joe” Bush

Brainerd-born right-hander who won a title with the 1923 Yankees — the first championship club of Babe Ruth’s New York years.

Bank clerk, 1933

Zane Smith

Nineteen-year-old messenger who walked into the First National Bank as Nelson’s gang held it. He was punched, dragged across the floor, and later became the last living witness to the robbery.

Philanthropist

Leon E. Lum

Young attorney turned civic donor who gave the city wooded parkland on Rice Lake — today’s Lum Park — expanding a public shoreline that still belongs to Brainerd.

Track builder

George Montgomery

Retired Northwest Airlines pilot who carved Donnybrooke Speedway through the woods in 1968. Jerry Hansen bought it in 1973 and renamed it Brainerd International Raceway.

A condensed century

Timeline

1805

Pike on Christmas Day

U.S. Army Lt. Zebulon Pike camps near the future townsite while searching for the headwaters of the Mississippi.

1870

The Crossing is chosen

Northern Pacific president J. Gregory Smith picks a Mississippi crossing seven miles north of Crow Wing Village. The settlement is first called Ogemaqua, then Brainerd.

1871

A bridge, then a town

The wooden railroad trestle — The Crossing — is finished on March 8. The first passenger train follows within days.

1873

City status

Brainerd is organized as a city on March 6. Gregory Square is reserved at the center of the plat.

1875

The trestle falls

On July 27 the wooden bridge collapses under a freight train. The locomotive and 22 cars plunge into the river. Four people die.

1876

Charter revoked

After voters elect handyman Thomas Lanihan mayor instead of Judge C. B. Sleeper, the legislature strips the city charter for six years.

1903

Paper arrives

Northwest Paper Company builds Brainerd’s first pulp mill, beginning a century of papermaking beside the river.

1921

The concrete tower

Brainerd finishes what is described as the nation’s first all-concrete municipal water tower — still the city’s signature landmark.

1922

Railroad strike

A nationwide shopmen’s strike idles the Northern Pacific works. In a town where most paychecks came from the railroad, the blow is civic as well as industrial.

1933

Baby Face Nelson

On October 23, Lester Gillis — Baby Face Nelson — and his gang rob the First National Bank of about $32,000. No one is killed.

1950

Paul comes home

A 26-foot talking Paul Bunyan, bought after the Chicago Railroad Fair, opens Paul Bunyan Land and turns lumber lore into a lakes-country brand.

1968

Donnybrooke

A raw 3-mile road course is cut through woods south of North Long Lake. It will become Brainerd International Raceway.

Still here

What remains

The Northern Pacific is gone as a name. BNSF still works a smaller shop. The brick shops district is on the National Register. The Carnegie library of 1904 and the Crow Wing County Courthouse still hold their corners. Gregory Square is still a square. The Mississippi is still the reason anyone stopped.

Brainerd today is the seat of Crow Wing County and the commercial heart of a lakes region that fills every July. About 14,400 people live in the city proper. The rest of the story happens on weekends, on docks, and on the long paved line of the Paul Bunyan State Trail.
